1. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)

What it is & Why it's used: RMSE calculates the average distance between the model's predicted exam score and the actual score, heavily penalizing large outliers because the errors are squared. It is the gold standard for continuous prediction models.

The Verdict: Linear Regression wins by achieving the lowest RMSE (1.80). Because the dataset has strong linear trends (like study hours increasing scores), it maps the trajectory perfectly. Decision Trees perform worst (RMSE 3.53) because they overfit the training data and fail to generalize on unseen test data.

2. R-Squared (R²) Variance Score

What it is & Why it's used: R² measures the proportion of variance in the final exam score that can be explained by the student's input features. A higher percentage (closer to 1.0) means the model accurately captures the underlying trends.

The Verdict: Linear Regression is best, explaining 77.0% of the variance. Advanced ensemble models like XGBoost and Random Forest struggle slightly (67%) because they partition data into step-based boundaries, making it harder for them to extrapolate smooth, continuous linear slopes.

3. Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

What it is & Why it's used: MAE provides the average absolute distance between predicted and actual scores. Unlike RMSE, it treats all errors equally without severely punishing outliers. It gives us a straightforward "points off" metric to understand basic accuracy.

The Verdict: Consistent with previous metrics, Linear Regression provides the tightest absolute fit. The predictions are consistently closer to the real exam scores point-for-point than any complex tree-based architecture.

4. Mean Squared Error (MSE)

What it is & Why it's used: MSE is the foundational calculation for RMSE. It represents the raw squared variance of prediction errors. While harder to interpret in direct "exam points", it is essential for algorithmic gradient descent to optimize model weights.

The Verdict: The MSE chart decisively illustrates the mathematical gap between Linear mapping (lowest variance) and Tree-based splitting algorithms, which generate high variance on linear datasets.

5. Overall Cross-Validation Score Comparison

Understanding 5-Fold Cross-Validation

What it is: Instead of relying on a single test score, the dataset is divided into 5 equal chunks (folds). The model trains on 4 chunks and tests its predictions on the 1 remaining chunk. This entire process repeats 5 times so that every chunk acts as a test set exactly once.

Why we use it: Averaging the scores across all 5 distinct test runs mathematically eliminates the chance of getting a "lucky split." It guarantees that the metrics displayed above represent the model's true, unbiased ability to forecast new student performance in the real world.
